The History of BJJ

From Japan to Brazil to the World

The Primary Strategy of BJJ

Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu places great emphasis on ground fighting techniques (ne waza in Japan). The primary strategy is to take the opponent to the ground and force them to fight you there. The theory is simple and proven: if you are skilled at fighting on the ground and your attacker is not, your attacker will be virtually at your mercy once you have them on the ground.

More than 80% of all street fights end up on the ground according to experts including Rorion Gracie. This makes ground fighting skills essential for any well-rounded martial artist.

The Importance of Leverage

Virtually every technique in Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu makes use of leverage. To excel in jiu-jitsu you must understand what leverage really is and how it works within the context of the art.

When you learn the principles of leverage, you will know how and why your techniques work or don't work. Learn the principles and you will be able to create your own submissions and defend against your opponents' submission attempts far more effectively.

1882

Jigoro Kano Creates Judo

In Tokyo, Japan, Professor Jigoro Kano develops Kodokan Judo, emphasizing throws and ground fighting. This becomes the foundation for Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu. Kano's philosophy focused on maximum efficiency and mutual welfare.

1897

Mitsuyo Maeda Begins Training

Mitsuyo Maeda (Count Koma), a direct student of Kano, begins training with Kodokan Judo. He would later travel the world challenging fighters and proving Judo's effectiveness in real combat situations.

1904

Maeda Travels to United States

Maeda travels to the United States with instructor Tsunejiro Tomita. They demonstrate their art at West Point and perform for President Theodore Roosevelt at the White House.

1909-1914

Maeda Arrives in Brazil

Maeda arrives in Brazil on a mission to help establish a Japanese colony. In Belém, he meets businessman Gastão Gracie who helps him establish the colony and returns the favor by having Maeda teach his son.

1916-1921

Carlos Gracie Studies Under Maeda

At age 15, Carlos Gracie begins studying under Mitsuyo Maeda, continuing until he was 21. This training took place at a time when it was considered improper for a Japanese instructor to teach a non-Japanese person. Carlos was never defeated in his fights.

1925

First Gracie Academy Opens

Carlos Gracie opens the first Gracie Academy in Rio de Janeiro with his younger brother Hélio as co-instructor. This marks the official beginning of organized BJJ instruction and the Gracie family's commitment to teaching the art.

1920s-1940s

Hélio Gracie Refines the Art

Hélio Gracie, smaller and weaker than most opponents, adapted the techniques to emphasize leverage and timing over strength. He developed many submissions and guard positions that became signature BJJ techniques, making the art accessible to smaller practitioners.

1951

Helio vs. Kado Fight

In July 1951, Helio Gracie faced 5th-degree black belt judo fighter Kado. Helio dominated the fight and eventually choked Kado into unconsciousness, becoming a national hero of Brazil and proving the effectiveness of his modified techniques.

1951

Helio vs. Masahiko Kimura

After defeating Kado, Helio challenged the team again. Masahiko Kimura accepted. After a grueling battle in front of 20,000 fans, Kimura broke Helio's elbow with an ude-garami arm lock now known as "The Kimura." Even with the broken elbow, Helio refused to give up.

1982

Rolls Gracie's Death

Rolls Gracie, son of Carlos Gracie, died in a hang gliding accident at age 31. He made major contributions to BJJ including advancing the open guard, leg locks, and takedowns. He promoted Romero "Jacare" Cavalcanti to black belt before his death.

1985

Alliance Team Formed

Three years after Rolls' death, Romero "Jacare" Cavalcanti and his top students created the Alliance Team. The mission was to carry on the legacy of Rolls Gracie. Today Alliance is home to many world champions including Fabio Gurgel, Marcello Garcia, and Roberto Traven.

1993

UFC 1 - Royce Gracie Dominates

Royce Gracie, representing Gracie Jiu-Jitsu, wins the first Ultimate Fighting Championship despite being the smallest fighter. His victories demonstrated BJJ's effectiveness against strikers and wrestlers, sparking global interest in the art and changing martial arts forever.

1994-2000

Global Expansion

Following UFC success, BJJ schools opened worldwide. The International Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu Federation (IBJJF) was founded in 1994, establishing standardized rules and tournaments. BJJ became a required skill for MMA fighters worldwide.

2000s-Present

Modern Era & No-Gi Evolution

No-Gi BJJ exploded in popularity with organizations like ADCC. New techniques emerged including leg lock systems, berimbolo, and guard pulling strategies. The art continues to evolve with contributions from competitors worldwide including Gordon Ryan and others.

Techniques, Tactics, and Strategy

Techniques: The systematic procedures by which tasks are accomplished (specific moves like armbar, triangle)

Tactics: The technique of deploying and directing maneuvers against an enemy (combining techniques for attacking, counterattacking, defending)

Strategy: The science and art of overall planning and conduct of combat operations (planning ahead, understanding opponent personality types)

Advanced jiu-jitsu fighters are like generals who shoot. They plan, they maneuver, and they execute. Understanding all three levels is essential for championship-level performance.

Understanding Fighter Personality Types

According to psychological research and martial arts analysis, there are four main fighter personality types that manifest in BJJ:

⚡

Aggressive

Attacks and charges constantly at full power. Prefers pressure passing and constant offense.

🎭

Deceptive

Uses timing, fakes, and quick movements assertively to gain advantage. Excels at sweeps and transitions.

🛡️

Defensive

Defends constantly, stalls a lot, avoids submissions, escapes danger, and attacks rarely when safe.

🎯

Interceptive

Sets traps and uses counterattacks frequently. Excels at guard retention and submission setups.

Strategic Insight: Every fighter has a natural tendency toward one or two styles. Understanding your opponent's type allows you to adapt your strategy accordingly. Advanced fighters can adapt their style to accommodate any opponent type.

The 18 Ground Fighting Conditions

Every Position You Must Master

The 18 Ground Fighting Conditions

There are nine primary situations that are most common to Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu and ground fighting in general. Each of these nine situations is fluid and has many variations. The successful ground fighter must have more than a few throws and a handful of submission techniques. The successful fighter needs a methodology for attacking, defending, counterattacking, and outsmarting different opponents in a wide variety of situations.

Key Insight: Each situation has two aspects (top and bottom), giving us 18 total conditions that every BJJ practitioner must master.

1. Standing Attacks

Position: Both fighters on feet

Focus: Takedowns, clinches, distance management

Key Skills: Single-leg, double-leg, hip throws, collar ties

2. Standing Counters

Position: Defending takedowns

Focus: Sprawl, underhooks, framing

Key Skills: Sprawl-and-brawl, whizzer, head control

3. Closed Guard (Top)

Position: On top, opponent has legs locked around waist

Focus: Posture control, breaking guard, passing

Key Skills: Posture up, knee cut, stack pass

4. Closed Guard (Bottom)

Position: On bottom, legs locked around opponent's waist

Focus: Submissions, sweeps, control

Key Skills: Armbar, triangle, omoplata, scissor sweep

5. Open Guard (Top)

Position: On top, opponent's legs are open

Focus: Passing, controlling distance

Key Skills: Pressure passing, knee slice, Torreando

6. Open Guard (Bottom)

Position: On bottom, legs not locked

Focus: Framing, creating space, submissions

Key Skills: De la Riva, butterfly guard, X-guard

7. Half Guard (Top)

Position: Controlling one of opponent's legs

Focus: Finishing the pass, controlling posture

Key Skills: Knee cut, heavy pressure, underhook

8. Half Guard (Bottom)

Position: One leg trapped by opponent

Focus: Escaping, sweeping, creating openings

Key Skills: Shrimp, electric chair, Z-guard

9. Side Control (Top)

Position: Across opponent's torso, perpendicular

Focus: Maintaining pressure, setting up submissions

Key Skills: Kimura, Americana, arm trap

10. Side Control (Bottom)

Position: On bottom, opponent across chest

Focus: Escaping, framing, regaining guard

Key Skills: Elbow-knee escape, shrimp, framing

11. North-to-South (Top)

Position: Head-to-head with opponent

Focus: Pressure, chokes, transitions

Key Skills: North-south choke, arm trap, mount transition

12. North-to-South (Bottom)

Position: Opponent's head at your head

Focus: Creating space, escaping, framing

Key Skills: Frame, shrimp, bridge and roll

13. Knee-in-the-Belly (Top)

Position: Knee driving into opponent's abdomen

Focus: Control, pressure, transition to mount

Key Skills: Knee slide, mount transition, pressure

14. Knee-in-the-Belly (Bottom)

Position: Opponent's knee in your belly

Focus: Removing knee, escaping, framing

Key Skills: Frame, shrimp, knee removal

15. Mount (Top)

Position: Sitting on opponent's torso

Focus: Maximum control, submissions, strikes

Key Skills: Armbar, triangle, mounted kimura, Ezequiel choke

16. Mount (Bottom)

Position: Opponent sitting on your torso

Focus: Protecting neck, escaping, framing

Key Skills: Elbow-knee escape, shrimp, bridging

17. Back (Top)

Position: Behind opponent, hooks in

Focus: Back control, chokes, finishing

Key Skills: Rear naked choke, bow and arrow, seatbelt grip

18. Back (Bottom)

Position: Opponent behind you, hooks in

Focus: Protecting neck, removing hooks, escaping

Key Skills: Hand fight, hook removal, rolling escape

Why Master All 18 Conditions?

Some of the 18 conditions describe dominant situations such as throwing your opponent to the ground or being mounted on your opponent's chest. Some describe defensive situations such as being under the mount or having an opponent on your back. Some are both offensive and defensive, such as working from the open guard and its many variations.

The Point: The successful ground fighter must have more than a few throws and a handful of submission techniques. The successful fighter needs a methodology for attacking, defending, counterattacking, and outsmarting different opponents in a wide variety of situations. In other words, the successful fighter needs a strategic plan for how to work in all 18 conditions common to the game.

Classification Domains

Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu approaches martial arts from four different domains, or classifications:

  • Grappling with the Gi: Teaches how to deal with clothing during a fight. Clothing can be used as a weapon on the street. Training with the gi adds something to your no-gi game.
  • Grappling without the Gi: Teaches you to react more quickly and to use positional holds for leverage rather than relying on cloth. Essential for dealing with attackers wearing sleeveless shirts, shorts, or flimsy attire.
  • NHB Fighting without the Gi: Also known as no-rules, vale tudo, or mixed martial arts (MMA). Adds the element of striking from standing and grounded positions.
  • Self-Defense: Includes specialized techniques for defending oneself on the street, including one-on-one empty-hand techniques, defending against multiple attackers, and defending against weapon attacks.

Important: To be a well-rounded jiu-jitsu fighter, it is important to train in all four classifications.

Closed Guard Retention
Beginner

Learn to maintain your guard position and prevent opponents from passing.

  1. Keep your feet on opponent's hips to control distance
  2. Use your hands to control their posture (collar and sleeve grips)
  3. Frame against their shoulders to create space
  4. Move your hips to maintain angle and prevent pressure
  5. Use knee shields when necessary to block passes
Tip: Stay active - don't let them settle into your guard! Constant movement prevents them from establishing control.
Mount Escape (Shrimp)
Beginner

Escape from being mounted by shrimping to create space.

  1. Trap one of opponent's arms to prevent them from posturing
  2. Grab their far sleeve or collar for control
  3. Shrimp your hips away from them to create space
  4. Bring your knee up to create frame against their body
  5. Roll to side and recover guard or stand up
Tip: Keep your elbows tight to protect your neck! Don't let them isolate your arms for submissions.
Basic Armbar from Guard
Beginner

Submit your opponent from closed guard using an armbar.

  1. Control one of their arms with both hands (wrist and elbow)
  2. Break their posture down by pulling head toward you
  3. Thrust your hip toward their face to create angle
  4. Open your guard and swing leg over their head
  5. Squeeze legs together and arch hips to finish
Tip: Angle your body 45 degrees for better leverage! This prevents them from rolling out.
Side Control Escape
Beginner

Escape from side control using framing and shrimping.

  1. Frame against their neck with near arm
  2. Frame against their hip with far arm
  3. Shrimp your hips away to create space
  4. Bring knee up to block their advance
  5. Reguard or stand up when space is created
Tip: Keep your elbows tucked! This protects against armbars and kimuras.
Triangle Choke
Intermediate

Finish with a triangle choke from guard position.

  1. Control opponent's posture with collar and sleeve grips
  2. Isolate one arm across your body
  3. Throw leg over shoulder and behind neck
  4. Catch ankle and pull down to secure position
  5. Angle body 45 degrees and squeeze knees together
  6. Pull head down while extending hips to finish
Tip: Cut the angle to 45 degrees for maximum pressure! This prevents them from posturing up.
Kimura from Side Control
Intermediate

Apply Kimura lock from side control position.

  1. Reach under opponent's arm from side control
  2. Grab their wrist with your near hand
  3. Step over their body with far leg
  4. Grab your own wrist (figure-four grip)
  5. Lift their arm and turn toward them
  6. Apply pressure by rotating hips
Tip: Keep their arm close to your body! This prevents them from escaping the lock.
Scissor Sweep
Intermediate

Sweep opponent from closed guard using scissor motion.

  1. Control opponent's posture with grips
  2. Isolate one of their arms
  3. Place legs in scissor position (one high, one low)
  4. Push with top leg while pulling with bottom leg
  5. Use momentum to roll them over
  6. Follow to mount or back control
Tip: Apply leverage when opponent is slightly imbalanced! This is the moment of maximum opportunity.
Rear Naked Choke
Intermediate

Finish with rear naked choke from back control.

  1. Secure seatbelt grip (one arm over, one under)
  2. Insert choking arm under opponent's chin
  3. Grab your own bicep with choking hand
  4. Free other hand and grab opponent's head
  5. Squeeze biceps together while pulling head
  6. Keep hooks in to prevent escape
Tip: Choke with your biceps, not your forearm! This creates proper blood choke pressure.
Berimbolo
Advanced

Dynamic inversion technique to take opponent's back from guard.

  1. Establish deep half guard or butterfly guard
  2. Control opponent's far leg and belt
  3. Initiate inversion by lifting hips
  4. Spin around opponent's body
  5. Secure back control with hooks
  6. Finish with rear naked choke or other submission
Tip: This requires flexibility and timing! Practice the motion slowly before adding speed.
Heel Hook (Inside)
Advanced

Dangerous leg lock targeting the knee ligaments.

  1. Secure opponent's leg in entanglement position
  2. Control their heel with both hands
  3. Rotate your body to create angle
  4. Apply rotational pressure to the heel
  5. Keep opponent's knee aligned to prevent injury
  6. Release immediately when opponent taps
DANGER: This technique can cause serious knee damage! Only practice with trusted partners who understand tap timing.
X-Guard Sweep
Advanced

Marcelo Garcia's signature sweep from X-guard position.

  1. Enter X-guard by trapping one of opponent's legs
  2. Control their posture with collar grip
  3. Use your legs to lift their base
  4. Push their standing leg while pulling upper body
  5. Roll them over to side or back
  6. Follow to mount or back control
Tip: Timing is everything! Execute when opponent is shifting weight.
Leg Drag Pass
Advanced

Advanced guard passing technique using leg drag.

  1. Break opponent's guard by controlling hips
  2. Grab opponent's far leg with near hand
  3. Drag their leg across your body
  4. Step over to side control position
  5. Secure position with pressure
  6. Transition to mount or back if opportunity arises
Tip: Maintain pressure throughout! Don't give them space to recover guard.
Clothing Grab Defense
Street

Defend against clothing grabs in street situations.

  1. Immediately control the grabbing hand
  2. Step toward the grab to reduce leverage
  3. Use their momentum against them
  4. Take them to ground if necessary
  5. Control and create escape opportunity
WARNING: Street situations are unpredictable! Prioritize escape over submission when possible.
Multiple Attacker Awareness
Street

Positioning and awareness for multiple attacker scenarios.

  1. Never let yourself be surrounded
  2. Keep one attacker between you and others
  3. Use environment to limit angles of attack
  4. Disengage and create distance when possible
  5. Escape is priority over engagement
WARNING: BJJ is less effective against multiple attackers! Situational awareness and escape are paramount.
Ground-and-Pound Defense
Street

Protect yourself from strikes while on the ground.

  1. Keep elbows tight to protect vital areas
  2. Frame against opponent's head to create space
  3. Shield your face with forearms
  4. Look for escape opportunities
  5. Stand up when possible to reset
WARNING: Ground fighting with strikes is dangerous! Create distance and escape when possible.
Weapon Defense Basics
Street

Basic principles for defending against weapons.

  1. Avoid confrontation when weapon is present
  2. If unavoidable, control the weapon arm immediately
  3. Use distance and angles to your advantage
  4. Create opportunity to escape
  5. Do not attempt complex techniques under pressure
WARNING: Weapon defense is extremely dangerous! Running away is always the best option.

Training Philosophy

According to Relson Gracie: "In order to be world champion, you've got to be like a Brazilian; train morning and afternoon, five days a week to be at top level, and you've got to have a good teacher for sure."

The exact number of hours required to become a champion is a matter of opinion, but everyone agrees that it takes a lot of heart, a ton of hard work, lots of mat time, and plenty of competition experience.

Mental Mastery: In one study, two men practiced free throws each night for a month, while a third only visualized shooting the ball. The first man visualized and practiced free throws, while the second man just shot the ball without visualizing. At the end of the month the most improved player was the one who both visualized and shot free throws. Coming in second was the man who simply visualized, and coming in last was the one who shot without visualizing. To be great — really great — you must give 100 percent of yourself, body and mind.

Belt Ranking System

The Journey from White to Red

The Journey from White to Red

The Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu belt system represents a journey of dedication, skill development, and personal growth. Unlike many martial arts, BJJ has relatively few belt colors, with significant time requirements between each rank. This ensures that each belt represents genuine competency and mastery.

Click on any belt below to learn more about the requirements and expectations for that rank.

White Belt
0-2 Years

White Belt - The Beginning

Duration: 0-2 years typical

Focus: Survival, fundamentals, and basic positions

Key Skills to Develop:

  • Learning to survive in bad positions
  • Basic guard retention
  • Fundamental escapes (mount, side control)
  • Basic submissions (armbar, triangle, rear naked choke)
  • Understanding positional hierarchy

Mindset: Focus on survival and defense. Don't worry about winning - focus on not losing. Learn to be comfortable in uncomfortable positions.

Blue Belt
2-4 Years

Blue Belt - The Foundation

Duration: 2-4 years typical

Focus: Building a complete game, guard development, and combinations

Key Skills to Develop:

  • Developing a reliable guard (closed, open, half)
  • Basic sweeps and guard passes
  • Combining techniques into sequences
  • Understanding leverage and timing
  • Beginning competition experience

Mindset: This is where you build your foundation. Expect to be tapped often by higher belts. Focus on understanding WHY techniques work, not just memorizing them.

Purple Belt
4-7 Years

Purple Belt - The Middle Path

Duration: 4-7 years typical

Focus: Refinement, personal style development, and teaching basics

Key Skills to Develop:

  • Developing a personal game and style
  • Advanced guard systems (De La Riva, X-guard, etc.)
  • Counter-attacks and chain wrestling
  • Ability to teach basic techniques to others
  • Competition success at intermediate level

Mindset: Purple belt is often considered the "make or break" rank. You should be able to handle most white and blue belts comfortably. Focus on refining your strengths and addressing weaknesses.

Brown Belt
7-10 Years

Brown Belt - The Expert

Duration: 7-10 years typical

Focus: Mastery, teaching ability, and competition excellence

Key Skills to Develop:

  • Near-complete technical mastery
  • Ability to teach all fundamental techniques
  • Advanced strategy and tactics
  • Consistent competition success
  • Leadership in the academy

Mindset: Brown belt is the final step before black. You should be able to dominate most purple belts and compete with black belts. Focus on consistency and preparing for the responsibility of black belt.

Black Belt
10+ Years

Black Belt - The Expert

Duration: 10+ years typical (average 10-15 years)

Focus: Teaching, competition, and art preservation

Key Skills to Develop:

  • Complete technical mastery
  • Ability to run an academy
  • Representing the art with integrity
  • Continued learning and innovation
  • Mentoring the next generation

Mindset: Black belt is not the end - it's a new beginning. You represent the art and carry responsibility for its preservation and growth. Continue to learn, innovate, and give back to the community.

Coral Belt
Red & Black

Coral Belt - The Master

Requirements: 7th degree black belt (typically 25-30+ years training)

Significance: The coral belt (red and black) represents mastery beyond the black belt. It is awarded to 7th degree black belts who have dedicated decades to the art.

Notable Coral Belts:

  • Helio Gracie (awarded 7th degree)
  • Carlson Gracie
  • Rickson Gracie
  • Rorion Gracie

Mindset: Coral belt holders are living legends who have shaped the art. They represent the highest level of dedication and contribution to Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu.

Red Belt
Grandmaster

Red Belt - The Grandmaster

Requirements: 9th and 10th degree black belt (typically 40-50+ years training)

Significance: The red belt is reserved for the founders and pioneers of the art. It represents the highest possible achievement in Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu.

Notable Red Belts:

  • Carlos Gracie Sr. (10th degree - founder)
  • Helio Gracie (10th degree - co-founder)
  • Carlson Gracie (9th degree)

Mindset: Red belt holders are the architects of Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu. Their legacy extends beyond technique to the philosophy and culture of the art itself.

Stripe System

Within each belt rank (except red), practitioners earn stripes (usually 4) before advancing to the next belt. Stripes represent progress within a belt level and are typically awarded every 6-12 months for colored belts.

Stripe Requirements:

  • Consistent training attendance
  • Technical improvement
  • Time in grade
  • Competition participation (optional but encouraged)
  • Character and attitude

Children's Belt System

Children (under 16) follow a different belt system with additional colors to provide more frequent progression and motivation:

  • White Belt
  • Grey Belt (with white, solid, or black bar)
  • Yellow Belt (with white, solid, or black bar)
  • Orange Belt (with white, solid, or black bar)
  • Green Belt (with white, solid, or black bar)

At age 16, children transition to the adult belt system, typically starting at white or blue belt depending on their previous rank and skill level.

Whether you are a beginner or an accomplished veteran of the art, when you finish reading this book, your life as a martial artist will be forever changed.

Apply what you learn and you will find yourself transformed from follower to leader, from private to general, from technician to strategist.

The Spirit of Rolls Gracie

Rolls Gracie was a man of action to be sure, and he finished many fights, yet he was a truly good and generous person. Through his teaching and his example, Rolls influenced many of today's best — and best-known — jiu-jitsu fighters and coaches. "Rolls later passed his personal developments on to his brothers and cousins, giving the younger generations a great push forward. His influence on Rickson and Royler is unmistakable. He also taught Carlos Jr., Rorion, and Crolin."

His legacy lives on in the spirit of innovation, technical excellence, and generosity that defines the modern art.
